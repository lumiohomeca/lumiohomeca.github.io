Level 01 · Beginner

Build the foundation.

A clear starting curriculum before choosing your first serious watch.

BEGINNER · LESSON 01

How a watch keeps time.

A movement is the engine. It stores or receives energy, divides time into measured intervals, and moves the hands. The big question is where that energy comes from.

BEGINNER · LESSON 02

Mechanical, automatic or quartz?

A manual mechanical watch is wound by hand. An automatic winds through wrist motion. Quartz uses a battery and a vibrating crystal for accuracy. None is universally “best”—the right one depends on use and feeling.

BEGINNER · LESSON 03

What specifications matter.

Case diameter affects fit; sapphire resists scratches; water resistance describes real limits; and a seller’s warranty and returns matter as much as the reference itself.

BEGINNER · LESSON 04

Water resistance, without the confusion.

“Water resistant” is not a single promise. Read the exact rating, the crown design and the manufacturer’s instructions. Heat, steam and an open crown can compromise a watch even when its rating sounds reassuring.

BEGINNER · LESSON 05

Finding the right fit.

Diameter is only the start. Consider lug-to-lug length, thickness, bracelet taper and your wrist shape. A well-proportioned 38 mm watch can feel more substantial than a poorly fitted 42 mm watch.

BEGINNER · LESSON 06

Care from day one.

Keep the crown secure, wipe the case and bracelet after wear, and do not treat magnetic fields or impacts casually. Good care protects both daily enjoyment and eventual resale condition.

BEGINNER · LESSON 07

What the crown does.

The crown sets the time and often the date; on some watches it also winds the movement. Pull it gently and only as far as needed. A screw-down crown must be fully secured before exposure to water.

BEGINNER · LESSON 08

Power reserve, in plain language.

Power reserve is how long a fully wound mechanical watch can run without more winding or wrist movement. It helps you understand whether a watch will keep running between wears.

BEGINNER · LESSON 09

Crystal: mineral, sapphire or acrylic?

Sapphire is highly scratch resistant; mineral is common and practical; acrylic has vintage charm but marks more easily. Each has a purpose—know which one you are buying and how it will age.

BEGINNER · LESSON 10

Date windows and calendar care.

Many mechanical watches have recommended times when you should not quick-set the date. Check the manual for the exact movement, and never force a crown that does not turn smoothly.

BEGINNER · LESSON 11

Why lume matters.

Lume is the luminous material that makes hands and indices visible in low light. It is useful on field and dive-inspired watches, but its brightness and longevity vary by formula and application.

BEGINNER · LESSON 12

Bracelet basics.

A good bracelet should sit securely without pinching, and should allow small adjustments as your wrist changes through the day. Fit matters more than a few extra links or a dramatic clasp.

BEGINNER · LESSON 13

Reading the caseback.

The caseback often lists water resistance, movement information, country markings and a reference or serial number. It is a useful starting point for manuals, service and product verification.

BEGINNER · LESSON 14

Your first watch rotation.

One versatile watch is enough to start. If you add a second, choose a different role—perhaps a refined everyday piece beside something casual or more water-ready—rather than duplicating the same use.

Level 02 · Intermediate

Develop your point of view.

Learn to read a watch’s design language and position with more confidence.

INTERMEDIATE · LESSON 01

Dress, field, diver or chronograph?

Dress watches are restrained; field watches favour legibility; divers prioritise water resistance; chronographs add timing functions. Categories help explain a watch’s intended character.

INTERMEDIATE · LESSON 02

What a brand can signal.

Brands differ in design language, heritage, movement choices, service network and price positioning. A logo is not a specification; look at the exact reference and what it gives you.

INTERMEDIATE · LESSON 03

Complications, explained simply.

A complication is any function beyond displaying hours, minutes and seconds: date, GMT, chronograph, calendar or moonphase. More functions can add usefulness, but also change thickness, cost and servicing needs.

INTERMEDIATE · LESSON 04

Steel, titanium, gold and ceramic.

Material changes more than appearance. Steel is versatile, titanium is light, gold has warmth and presence, and ceramic offers exceptional scratch resistance. The best choice is the one that suits how you actually wear it.

INTERMEDIATE · LESSON 05

Straps change the whole watch.

A bracelet makes a watch more architectural; leather can make it quieter and more formal; rubber shifts it toward active use. Learn the lug width and you can make one good watch feel like several.

INTERMEDIATE · LESSON 06

Dial anatomy and legibility.

Indices, hands, lume, minute track and date placement decide whether a dial is instantly readable. The strongest designs balance information with calm—especially in daily wear.

INTERMEDIATE · LESSON 07

What a bezel can do.

Bezels can measure elapsed time, track a second time zone or simply frame a dial. A rotating bezel is functional; a fixed bezel is often about proportion and visual character.

INTERMEDIATE · LESSON 08

Understanding accuracy.

Quartz is generally more accurate than mechanical. Mechanical watches trade absolute precision for craft, movement and tactile appeal. Judge accuracy against the movement type and the maker’s stated tolerance.

INTERMEDIATE · LESSON 09

How to read a reference number.

The exact reference tells you the case, dial, bracelet and variation—not just the collection. It is the safest way to compare specifications, price and availability across different sellers.

INTERMEDIATE · LESSON 10

Buying online with discipline.

Verify the reference, seller identity, return terms, warranty territory and final Canadian price. Product titles can be incomplete; the reference and written terms are what protect your purchase.

INTERMEDIATE · LESSON 11

How a watch wears with style.

Think about cuff clearance, colour palette, surface finish and occasion. A versatile watch does not need to disappear—it needs to complement the rest of the outfit without fighting it.

Level 03 · Collector

Choose with context.

A collector’s habit is comparing the complete reference, not simply the name on the dial.

COLLECTOR · LESSON 01

Choose with context.

Start with budget, wrist size and the occasions you dress for. Then compare exact references, current price and seller terms—not just a brand name.

COLLECTOR · LESSON 02

Service is part of ownership.

Mechanical watches need periodic professional attention, while quartz watches need battery and seal care. Before buying, look for service access, expected costs and the strength of the warranty in Canada.

COLLECTOR · LESSON 03

Condition, completeness and provenance.

Original parts, a clear seller history, box and papers, and honest condition notes all help you evaluate a watch. Documentation cannot replace judgment, but it makes comparison much more disciplined.

COLLECTOR · LESSON 04

Value is not the same as resale.

A watch can be excellent value because it fits your life, wears beautifully and is easy to service. Future resale is uncertain—treat it as a secondary consideration, never as a guarantee.

COLLECTOR · LESSON 05

Building a focused collection.

Start with a point of view: one era, one function, a wardrobe of different roles, or a material you love. Focus makes each addition more intentional and avoids buying the same watch repeatedly in disguise.

COLLECTOR · LESSON 06

Vintage: charm and responsibility.

Vintage watches can carry immense character, but they may have moisture damage, replaced parts or limited service support. Research the exact reference and buy condition first, story second.

COLLECTOR · LESSON 07

Polishing changes a case.

Polishing can refresh superficial marks, but aggressive work can soften original edges, alter finishing and reduce definition. When assessing a pre-owned watch, look at the shape as closely as the dial.

COLLECTOR · LESSON 08

Movement calibres matter.

A calibre can indicate architecture, power reserve, repairability and access to parts. Learn the movement name alongside the model name—two watches that look similar can offer very different ownership experiences.

COLLECTOR · LESSON 09

Insurance and secure storage.

As a collection grows, document serials, purchase records and photographs. Consider suitable insurance and keep watches away from moisture, magnets and casual access—care is part of collecting.

COLLECTOR · LESSON 10

When to walk away.

Walk away when a seller cannot confirm the reference, condition story changes, images are inconsistent, or the price feels too good without a clear explanation. Patience is one of a collector’s best tools.
